Chauffeur 101

a different job from driving fast — you are judged on what the person behind you feels, and it is measured, not asserted

1. The whole job in one number

A racing driver is judged on lap time. A chauffeur is judged on JERK — the rate at which acceleration changes, in m/s³. Not how hard you accelerate: how fast the hardness changes. A passenger cannot feel a steady 0.2 g. They feel the instant it arrives and the instant it stops, and if they are reading, or pouring something, or asleep, that is the only thing about your driving they will ever notice.

Chauffeur Mode scores you live on three things and nothing else: peak jerk, peak lateral g and whether you stopped at every red. Both numbers come straight off the running integrator — jerk is the derivative of the acceleration the physics is already producing, and lateral g is v × yaw rate / 9.81. Anything over 2.5 m/s³ or 0.30 g sideways is marked, because that is roughly where a person stops being able to ignore the car. Run one red and the grade is DISMISSED, immediately. You cannot talk your way to a good mark.

2. Reading the lights, early

Every junction here has a real name and a real signal on it, and every other car obeys the same one — the phase is a pure function of route time, so the taxi beside you and the bus in front are reading the clock you are reading. Nobody gets a private light. The binnacle names the next junction, its colour, and the seconds left on it.

That countdown is the entire skill. If a light is red with eight seconds left and you are 120 metres away, you do not brake — you lift, and arrive at it as it turns green having never touched the brake pedal. Braking is what you do when you failed to look far enough ahead. New York's lights are deliberately set on a green wave: hold the limit exactly and you will not stop once. London's are not, and never have been.

Amber is not "go faster". Amber means stop if you can do it comfortably, and in this car you almost always can.

3. A junction is a decision, not a corner

Choose London in Navigation and there is no route at all: you are on the real road network, twenty kilometres round Charing Cross, and every junction offers the roads it really has. Nothing is recalculating and nothing is going to pull you back onto a line, because there is no line.

The car goes where the wheel is pointing. From about two hundred metres out the binnacle lists what this junction offers — ← ↑ → with the road names — and highlights the one you are currently asking for; a fingerpost on the kerb says the same thing in the world. Turn the wheel and the highlight moves. You may change your mind right up to the stop line.

Lane centring is a separate switch from the chauffeur and it starts off, because a car that steers itself while you are choosing a turning is a car arguing with you. When it is on, it holds the real lane you are in — and it lets go entirely for the last forty-six metres of a road.

4. The line that keeps the back flat

The racing line and the chauffeur's line are not the same line and are sometimes opposites. A racing driver clips the apex to carry speed. You go wide of the apex and slow earlier, because the rear seat is a long way behind the front axle — 2.1 metres behind it in a Phantom — and everything you do at the front is amplified back there. A flick that feels tidy from the driver's seat is a shove in the ribs three rows of leather away.

One steering input per corner. Turn in once, hold, unwind once. A correction mid-corner is two events where there should have been none.

5. Stopping — and where the rear door lands

Release the brake as you stop. The classic mistake is holding constant pressure to a halt, which makes the car pitch back on its springs and nod — and the nod is what wakes people. Bleed the pressure off over the last two metres and the car settles instead of rocking.

And stop in the right place. Your passenger uses the rear door. At the hotel the carpet is laid for the rear door, not for you — which means the car stops with the front wheels past the mark, not on it.

6. The arrival

The last hundred metres are the only ones anybody watching remembers. Walking pace onto the forecourt, straight, square to the entrance, and stopped before the doors open — the coach doors on this car are hinged at the back and open into the wind, so they are opened stationary or not at all.

The White Palace route exists to practise exactly this and nothing else: four kilometres of gravel avenue where the only hard part is the last two hundred metres.

7. This car: 2,560 kg and 900 Nm at 1,700

The Phantom is built to make this job easy and you should let it. All 900 Nm is there at 1,700 rpm — just above idle — so you never need the revs, and the satellite-aided transmission reads the road ahead and picks the gear before the corner arrives so that you do not have to. A Phantom that is audible is a Phantom being driven badly, which is why there is no tachometer on it and never has been.

2,560 kg is the other half. It is what makes the car so calm over bad surfaces, and it is also what will punish a late decision: at 50 km/h this car needs real distance to stop softly. Everything above is downstream of that one number.
