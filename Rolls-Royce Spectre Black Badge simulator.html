Chauffeur 101

a different job from driving fast — you are being judged on what the person behind you feels, not on the clock

1. The whole job in one number

A racing driver is judged on lap time. A chauffeur is judged on JERK — the rate at which acceleration changes, measured in m/s³. Not how hard you accelerate: how fast the hardness changes. A passenger cannot feel a steady 0.2 g. They feel the instant it arrives and the instant it stops, and if they are reading, or pouring something, or asleep, that is the only thing about your driving they will ever notice.

Learning Mode scores you live on three things and nothing else: peak jerk, peak lateral g, and whether you stopped at every red. Anything over 2.5 m/s³ or 0.30 g sideways is marked, because those are roughly where a person stops being able to ignore the car.

2. Reading the lights, early

Every route here has real junctions with real names and real lights on them, and every other car obeys them too — the taxi beside you and the bus in front are reading the same clock you are. The HUD names the next junction, its colour and the seconds left.

That countdown is the entire skill. If a light is red with eight seconds left and you are 120 metres away, you do not brake — you lift, and arrive at it as it turns green having never touched the brake pedal at all. Braking is what you do when you failed to look far enough ahead. New York's lights are deliberately set on a green wave, ten seconds apart on a sixty-second cycle: hold the limit exactly and you will not stop once. London's are not, and never have been.

Amber is not "go faster". Amber means stop if you can do it comfortably, and in this car you almost always can.

3. The line that keeps the back flat

The racing line and the chauffeur's line are not the same line and are sometimes opposites. A racing driver clips the apex to carry speed. You go wide of the apex and slow earlier, because the rear seat is a long way behind the front axle — 1.8 metres behind it in a Spectre — and everything you do at the front is amplified back there. A quick flick that feels tidy from the driver's seat is a shove in the ribs three rows of leather away.

One steering input per corner. Turn in once, hold, unwind once. A correction mid-corner is two events where there should have been none.

4. Stopping — and where the door lands

Release the brake as you stop. The classic mistake is holding constant pressure to a halt, which makes the car pitch back on its springs and nod — and the nod is what wakes people. Bleed the pressure off over the last two metres and the car settles instead of rocking.

And stop in the right place. Your passenger uses the rear door. At the hotel, the carpet is laid for the rear door, not for you — which means the car stops with the front wheels past the mark, not on it. The Royal Hotel route ends on that carpet and Learning Mode will tell you where you actually put it.

5. The arrival

The last hundred metres are the only ones anybody watching remembers. Walking pace onto the forecourt, straight, square to the entrance, and stopped before the doors open — the coach doors on this car are hinged at the back and open into the wind, so they are opened stationary or not at all.

The White Palace route exists to practise exactly this and nothing else: four kilometres of gravel avenue where the only hard part is the last two hundred metres.

6. This car: 2,560 kg and 900 Nm at 1,700

The Spectre is built to make this job easy and you should let it. All 900 Nm is there at 1,700 rpm, so you never need the revs and the gearbox is set to upshift early — a Spectre that is audible is a Spectre being driven badly. The satellite-aided transmission reads the road ahead and picks the gear before the corner arrives, so that you do not have to.

2,560 kg is the other half. It is what makes the car so calm over bad surfaces, and it is also what will punish a late decision: at 50 km/h this car needs real distance to stop softly. Everything above is downstream of that one number.

Chauffeur Mode scores the drive live: peak jerk, peak lateral g, reds stopped for and reds run. It marks the signal countdown on the road ahead and shows where the rear door came to rest.
